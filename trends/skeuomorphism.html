📖 History & Theory

Skeuomorphism (Greek skeuos = tool, morphē = shape) is a design approach where digital elements imitate their real-world counterparts in appearance, texture and behaviour.

The iOS 6 era (2007–2013) — under Scott Forstall, Apple made skeuomorphism the gold standard. Notes used yellow legal-pad paper, Compass had a reflective brass face, iBooks shelved your library on wood, Podcasts was a reel-to-reel deck, and Game Center wore green casino felt.

Why it worked: users were crossing from physical to digital. A switch that looks like a real switch tells you exactly what it does — the metaphor is the affordance.

The fall: in 2013 Jony Ive launched iOS 7 flat. Critics had argued skeuomorphism was:

  • Artificially constraining — why must a digital book app look like wood?
  • Inconsistent — every app invented its own metaphor, with no unified language.
  • Heavy — complex textures and gradients cost GPU and battery.
  • Culturally dated — younger users had never seen a Rolodex or a tape reel.

Today: skeuomorphism still rules music software (virtual synths, mixing desks), game UIs and luxury apps — and its softer cousins neumorphism and Apple's Liquid Glass show depth is quietly back after years of flat extremism.
