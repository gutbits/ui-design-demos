Retro Terminal & CRT

Green-phosphor glow, scanlines, and the honest geometry of monospace.

For three decades the computer was the terminal: a glass tube glowing with monospaced text, one character cell at a time. The retro terminal aesthetic revives that look — phosphor green or amber on black, soft scanlines, a curved screen, and a cursor that never stops blinking. Below you'll find the history behind it, the visual principles that make it convincing, and several live, interactive demos you can actually type into.

Background

Where the Look Comes From

Before the bitmapped, windowed desktop, most people met a computer through a video display terminal (VDT): a keyboard and a cathode-ray-tube (CRT) screen wired to a distant mainframe or minicomputer. The terminal didn't run programs itself — it sent your keystrokes down the line and painted back whatever characters the host returned.

The DEC VT100 and its family

Digital Equipment Corporation's VT100, introduced in 1978, became the reference point for the whole genre. It standardized a set of escape sequences for moving the cursor, clearing the screen, and styling text — conventions later folded into the ANSI/ECMA terminal standards. Those same sequences still drive your modern terminal emulator today, which is why programs talk to xterm or the macOS Terminal as if they were addressing a VT100. IBM's terminals (the 3270 family for mainframes) and many others coexisted, but the VT-series DNA is what most "retro terminal" UIs are imitating.

Green vs. amber phosphor

A monochrome CRT glows in whatever color its phosphor coating emits when struck by the electron beam. Early terminals overwhelmingly used green phosphor (often the P1 type), because the human eye is most sensitive to green-yellow light, so the same beam energy looked brighter. Later, many manufacturers offered amber (P3-style) screens, which a lot of users found more comfortable for long sessions and which were marketed as reducing eye strain. Some monitors also used "paper-white" phosphors. There was never a single mandated standard — green simply came first and amber became the popular alternative, and that pairing is now the visual shorthand for "old computer."

Why monospace?

Character-cell terminals had no choice: the screen was a fixed grid of equal-width cells — typically 80 columns by 24 rows — and each cell held exactly one glyph. Monospaced type wasn't a style decision, it was the hardware. That fixed grid is also why so much early computing culture assumes 80-character lines, box-drawing characters, and tables aligned with spaces. The aesthetic inherits all of it: when you reach for a monospace font today, you're reaching for that grid.

Visual Language

Core Visual Principles

A convincing CRT look is more than green text on black. A handful of physical artifacts sell the illusion — and overdoing any of them tips into parody.

✱

Phosphor glow

Text isn't crisp — it bleeds light. A soft text-shadow halo in the phosphor color mimics how excited phosphor blooms beyond each character.

≡

Scanlines

The beam painted the image one horizontal line at a time, leaving faint dark gaps between lines. A subtle repeating gradient recreates them.

◠

Curvature & vignette

Real tubes bulged outward and dimmed toward the corners. A radial vignette and slightly rounded frame suggest the curved glass.

▮

The block cursor

A solid blinking block — not a thin I-beam — is the signature. It marks where the next character lands on the fixed cell grid.

≈

Flicker & persistence

Phosphor faded slowly and refresh was imperfect, so brightness wavered. A faint, occasional opacity flicker adds life without nausea.

▤

Monospace grid

Everything aligns to a character cell. Tables, ASCII art, and box-drawing characters lean into the grid rather than fighting it.

Application

When to Use It Today

The CRT look is a strong flavor. It carries connotations of hacking, hardware, nostalgia, and "this thing is for people who know what they're doing." That makes it a great fit for some products and a poor one for others.

Reach for it when…

  • Building developer tools, CLIs, status dashboards, or DevOps consoles where a terminal metaphor already fits.
  • Designing for games, sci-fi, cyberpunk, or horror — diegetic computer screens love this look.
  • You want an instant nostalgia or "underground/hacker" tone for a landing page, demoscene piece, or portfolio.
  • Content is genuinely text-first and benefits from a calm, monochrome, distraction-free surface.

Be cautious when…

  • The audience is broad or non-technical — the look can read as intimidating or broken.
  • You have dense, image-heavy, or data-visualization content that needs full color.
  • Brand trust matters (banking, healthcare, government) and "retro" undercuts perceived reliability.
  • The effects are decorative only — heavy scanlines and flicker over real UI quickly become fatiguing.
Caveats

Accessibility Caveats

The very artifacts that make CRT styling charming are also the ones most likely to hurt readability. Treat the aesthetic as a skin over an accessible foundation, never a replacement for one.

Things to watch

Motion sensitivity. Flicker, animated scanlines, and a constantly blinking cursor can trigger discomfort or be distracting. Always respect prefers-reduced-motion and offer a way to turn effects off — this page does both.

Contrast & glow. Glow halos and scanline gaps reduce effective contrast. Saturated green-on-black can also be hard to read at small sizes. Keep body text large, verify it still meets WCAG AA contrast, and don't rely on the glow for legibility.

Color alone. Monochrome phosphor means you can't use color to distinguish states. Convey meaning with text, symbols, position, or weight instead.

Real semantics underneath. A terminal "screen" should still be built from real, focusable, labeled HTML. The interactive demo above uses a genuine <input> with an accessible label, not a styled div.

Font rendering. Pixel and bitmap-style fonts can degrade badly at certain sizes or zoom levels. Test at multiple zoom levels, and consider a cleaner monospace fallback for long-form reading.

Under the Hood

How These Demos Work

Everything on this page is one self-contained file. The glow is a layered text-shadow; scanlines are a repeating-linear-gradient overlay; the drifting sweep and flicker are CSS keyframe animations; the curvature is a radial-gradient vignette plus inset shadow. The typewriter and the command line are short vanilla-JavaScript routines — a queue of characters revealed on a timer, and a tiny canned-response shell with command history. No frameworks, no canvas, no external scripts.
